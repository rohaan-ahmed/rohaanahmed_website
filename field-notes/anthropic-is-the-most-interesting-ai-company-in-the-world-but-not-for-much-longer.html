Anthropic is the most interesting AI company in the world, but not for much longer

Anthropic built its reputation doing things other technology companies wouldn’t. Now it’s preparing to become the sort of company where the shareholders may not let it.

I have a love-hate relationship with Anthropic.

See, until late 2025, I suspected that autonomous coding was a bridge too far for stochastic systems like Large Language Models. I had spent several weeks over the course of the previous year trying to get coding agents (although, I wasn’t calling them that at the time) to write relatively simple code for a toy algorithmic trading web application using existing APIs. The results were less than stellar — I could write and debug faster myself (with copious amounts of Stack Overflow) and produce cleaner, leaner code than the LLMs. Even though Github Copilot had shown signs that something useful could emerge, it was little more than autocomplete with a positive attitude at the time.

Then came Claude Opus 4.5

In December 2025, I built three custom apps for my Android phone in the same day using Claude Code: a habit tracker, a workout timer, and a kickboxing instructor — all customized to my particular requirements. For the first app, I reviewed the code after each prompt, but by the third, I was well and truly vibe-coding. I still use these apps today, as I first built them.

Since then, Claude has become something of a friend of mine — it’s the model family I use more than any other, both at work and in my personal life, for coding, research, learning, professional writing, and computer use.

But Claude hasn’t always been a trustworthy friend.

I’ve previously written about my frustration with Anthropic’s handling of Fable and Mythos, particularly its decision to gate-keep frontier capabilities, its silent model degradation, and data retention policies.

I’ve since come to better understand Anthropic’s position on safety. I believe its leadership sincerely worries about the consequences of their work. I respect that, even though I still disagree with many of their decisions, and refuse to get fully locked into their ecosystem.

To summarize: I love Anthropic’s technology, respect its researchers, believe its leadership is sincere, and don’t entirely trust the company.

I think that:

Anthropic is the most interesting company in AI

Obviously, they typically have the best models and some really good product design, but I think what makes them interesting is the work they choose to do and, occasionally, not to pursue.

Firstly, Constitutional AI

Anthropic pioneered Constitutional AI, challenging the prevailing reliance on human preference feedback with an approach that allowed models to evaluate their own behaviour against explicit humane principles, such as “Equal and fair treatment of all individuals”.

They even consulted theologians and priests on how to make Claude behave morally — because apparently two thousand years of debating morality makes them well prepared to write the system prompt.

Secondly, safety research that sometimes asks deeply inconvenient questions.

Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, interpretability work, investigations into deceptive behaviour, and model welfare research have made it unusually interesting to follow.

I find the model welfare research particularly fascinating. Anthropic is seriously investigating whether AI models might have experiences or interests that deserve moral consideration.

I am far from convinced that today’s models are conscious, but if we’re going to make AI answer customer service emails for eternity, we should probably establish whether it can feel pain or not.

Thirdly, Model Context Protocol

Anthropic created MCP, providing a common way for AI applications to connect to external tools and data, and then open-sourced it so everyone could build with it. Of course, by making it an open standard they helped ensure it became the de facto protocol. Not exactly charity, but it’s nice when a company’s attempt at domination produces something useful for everyone else.

Fourthly, Claude Code

Claude Code is perhaps the best demonstration of Anthropic’s ability to turn frontier research into extraordinarily useful products.

What I find particularly interesting is that it was first built to appeal to developers, hackers, and tinkerers. People building strange little tools, automating tedious work, and solving problems too specific for any sensible software company to bother with. So, people like me.

I strongly maintain that that early developer enthusiasm helped establish Claude Code as a serious product, including for enterprise customers.

Finally, the American federal government and the Mythos/Fable controversy

Anthropic’s confrontation with the US government over military applications and surveillance, alongside its decision to restrict access to some of its most capable models, revealed something unusual about the company: It was willing to make decisions that could cost it business to maintain its principles.

Taken together, these five examples describe an organization that has combined fundamental research, open technical contributions, commercial success, and an occasional willingness to tell powerful customers that they cannot have what they want.

That’s unusual in tech.

But an IPO will change that

As I write this in early November 2026, Anthropic is preparing to go public — although the recent American midterm elections and market downturn have complicated the timing and pricing.

I don’t begrudge the founders their IPO. Anthropic requires extraordinary amounts of capital to develop and operate its models, and its employees have created something enormously valuable. Many people will become spectacularly wealthy.

Good for them!

However, despite the founders’ best efforts to protect their voting power, the company will still be publicly traded.

As a public benefit corporation where the founders retain substantial voting control, its public shareholders will not be able to simply vote to change the company’s mission overnight. But, I think, the company will slowly transform into a traditional tech company in the long run.

The slow turn of the screw

Anthropic’s future shareholders do not have to share Anthropic’s vision. A researcher might consider understanding model consciousness a scientific priority, but a pension fund holding Anthropic shares is more concerned about the bottom line.

Neither one of them is wrong, they simply have different objectives.

There are several mechanisms through which those objectives will gradually change the organization.

Focus: Quarterly earnings calls, revenue forecasts, and operating margins will gradually dominate management decisions. Research that cannot demonstrate commercial value will become less important over time… imagine explaining to institutional investors that you’ve missed your earnings target because you’ve been investigating whether your chatbot is depressed.

Compensation: Anthropic employees already have equity incentives. Public trading will make their financial rewards more liquid and visibly connected to the company’s valuation.

Recruitment: As Anthropic grows, it will need more salespeople, product managers, financial specialists, and commercially focused executives. Naturally, any company increasingly recruiting for growth will eventually behave differently from one primarily recruiting to research AI.

Product decisions: Claude Code’s independent users will increasingly compete for attention with enterprise customers. Research into model interpretability will compete for funding with research that directly improves sellable products. And decisions to withhold models like Mythos will carry increasingly visible opportunity costs.

We’ve seen something similar before

Google went public in 2004 with its founders explicitly defending the possibility of sacrificing short-term profits to do what they believed was right. Over time, the company became a considerably more conventional commercial enterprise. Even its famous Don’t be evil motto was eventually moved to the last line of Google’s code of conduct.

Even OpenAI, Anthropic’s primary competitors, began as a nonprofit research organization and has since developed a commercial structure and enormous capital requirements that would have seemed rather extraordinary at its founding.

This is simply my prediction, not an obituary

There are strong arguments against everything I’ve just written, I am well aware. In fact, I would quite like to be wrong!

Anthropic has an unusual culture today — safety research, open contributions, and reputation among developers may be exactly what makes the company commercially successful. Even my example has a counter — Google routinely opens up its research to the world and it serves them well (mostly).

But, bottom-line, I think Anthropic 5-years post-IPO will be very different from Anthropic today.

I don’t see Anthropic abandoning their principles on Day 1. But a little more enterprise development this year. A little less speculative research next year. A product roadmap increasingly dominated by revenue growth. They may well have the best models, but they just won’t be quite as interesting.

And that would be a shame, because we’ve already got plenty of ordinary technology companies.
