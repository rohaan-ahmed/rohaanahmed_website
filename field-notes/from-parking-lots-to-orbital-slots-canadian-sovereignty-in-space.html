From Parking Lots to Orbital Slots — Canadian Sovereignty in Space

Canada has committed CAD 200 million to a launch pad in Nova Scotia and currently has zero rockets capable of using it. We have built the scenic parking lot, though, which is a start.

Disclaimer: No AI was used in the writing, editing, or reviewing of this article. I did, however, use GenerativeAI as a research assistant.

Earlier this month I had the opportunity to train alongside 60 other Air Force Junior Officers from NATO countries at a US Air National Guard and Reserve base in Boston. Amongst the many, many things I learned, perhaps the most prominent was a reinforcement of something I already kind of know… the US military is a behemoth.

One proof that space is infinite could be that it is large enough to hold the US military arsenal.

As someone who has been in the space industry for 12+ years, in Canada and in Europe, working with NASA and US Dept. of Defense, I know this to be true also of the US space sector. SpaceX has made getting to orbit cheaper, more frequent, and more dependable, and many countries, including Canada, have benefitted from that.

The problem, as I see it, is that the convenience has become a dependence.

Prior to COVID, if I wanted to eat fast food, I used to have to physically go and get it. This acted as a deterrent to consuming fast food every day — is a Shawarma really worth putting on shoes and a 20-minute round-trip in traffic? During COVID, I downloaded UberEats out of necessity, but it soon became a crutch. In 2024, at one point, my wife and I spent over $1,000 on UberEats in one month (and the scale showed it).

My lack of self-control is not the point here. The convenience of home delivery made it so that I had very little motivation to change my habits. Similarly, Canada has had very little motivation to develop sovereign space launch. Until now.

If you need someone else’s permission to use it, then it is not sovereign

These words were said by Brigadier General Christopher Horner, Commander of 3 Canadian Space Division (sometimes referred to as Canada’s Space Force) at a recent Canadian space conference I attended. He was speaking about the need for Canada to take ownership of its own sovereignty in space.

To me, sovereignty does not mean that every bolt needs to have a Maple Leaf on it. It means if and when Canada needs to reach orbit, we can do so ourselves, without approvals. The geopolitical situation is changing, and so are our alliances. As the US shifts its focus towards their internal needs, our dependency on them is beginning to become prohibitive.

Roughly 4 out of 5 Falcon 9 launches this year have carried Starlink satellites, future launches seem to be booked until 2028/2029, and rideshare pricing has climbed from roughly USD 5,000/kg in 2019 to about USD 7,000/kg today — the kind of demand not seen since the release of Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey in 70mm IMAX — I still can’t get a ticket

Commercially, therefore, the American option is oversubscribed and less available. Strategically, it now also depends on a relationship that is less assured than it was in the past. Either problem alone would be manageable, but together, it’s a red flag Canada cannot ignore.

Canada can build satellites and space robots, but can’t launch them

Canada was the third country in space, a fact most Canadians don’t know. Alouette 1 launched in 1962, designed and built in Canada, but launched on an American rocket, from an American site, with American approval. Canadarm and Canadarm2, two iconic Canadian space projects (one of which I had the privilege of working on), both rode on American vehicles. Sixty-four years, and the story remains the same.

We once had ambitions for a fully-fledged orbital launch capability. We gave it up for the convenience of relying on allies, in the same way we gave up the Avro Arrow and then spent sixty years buying American fighter jets.

One of Canada’s most significant space programs currently is Telesat Lightspeed, a constellation of satellites being designed to deliver high-speed and low-latency communications for government, defence, and commercial entities. The satellites will be operated by Telesat, a Canadian operator, and are being built by MDA Space, a Canadian systems integrator. So far so Canadian.

Here’s the problem: The constellation is expected to be deployed in 14 launches, all aboard SpaceX Falcon 9s from California and Florida.

Nobody really messed up here, there are just no credible Canadian launch options.

But now, Canada is actually building rockets…

… and I am excited!

Take my enthusiasm with the appropriate quantity of salt — not only am I a Reservist within 3 Canadian Space Division, I also happen to be on cordial terms with the leadership of some of Canada’s new rocket companies. I am not lobbying for any one company, I am arguing for a sovereign Canadian capability.

Ottawa has committed CAD 105 million to the “Launch the North” campaign, and granted NordSpace, Reaction Dynamics, and Canada Rocket Company CAD 8.3 million each for a first round of development. For reference, this is like having a competition between companies to build a skyscraper by handing each of them just enough cash to decorate a bedroom.

However, these companies are carrying on remarkably well through investments, and each is taking a different approach:

  • NordSpace is pursuing a broad vertically integrated approach, including its own spaceport and satellites, while investing in adjacent entities.
  • Reaction Dynamics is particularly interesting from a defence perspective because its propulsion concept is designed around dramatically reducing launch-site infrastructure.
  • Canada Rocket Company’s interesting angle is talent: bringing Canadian engineers home after years of watching them leave for American rocket companies.

To be clear, none of the three has reached orbit yet. NordSpace and Reaction Dynamics are targeting 2028, whereas CRC seems earlier in development. Still, for once, our problem isn’t that nobody is trying to build orbital rockets.

Ottawa has also rented a rocket parking space

One of my good Air Force friends lives a few kilometers from the Canso Spaceport in Nova Scotia, where Ottawa has committed CAD 200 million over 10 years for Maritime Launch Services’ launch pad. While Canada calls it “critical infrastructure”, my buddy calls it an “ocean-view parking lot”. He is genuinely excited about it and has already offered me a place to stay for the first launch.

It would make for a great Canadian sitcom, actually — a quiet Canadian town, with almost aggressively polite people, suddenly becomes one of NATO’s most important strategic sites (CBC, let’s work on this!)

In addition to NordSpace’s planned Atlantic Spaceport Complex in Newfoundland, Canada now has two potential spaceports and exactly zero orbital rockets. What could be more Canadian than that!

But how do we get to orbit?

If the spaceport is a parking lot, then somebody needs to bring the cars. Government grants can help build Proofs of Concepts, but it’s Purchase Orders that build industries. Canada should identify real government payloads now, fund demonstrations, and make future launch contracts conditional on technical milestones and successful flights.

If and when these companies reach orbit, Ottawa should not then begin a three-year process to discover what to do with this newfound capability. Just like rockets hit Maximum Dynamic Pressure (Max-Q) a minute-or-so after launch, government procurement hits Maximum Bureaucratic Pressure (Max-BS) sometime around the second fiscal year…

(This is a terrible joke and I am proud of it)

Let’s get ahead of this and have a funded plan for real launches.

Why continue to fund three companies?

Canada is not nearly as large as the US. We likely cannot sustain multiple launch providers based on domestic demand alone, and CAD 105 million split two or three-ways in future rounds is peanuts for rocket-building. We may not need three companies in 10 years, but we need the diversity of approach and competition now. Let the companies demonstrate capability, supported by investors and government funded milestones, and let future customers select which company/companies remain standing.

If the goal were to only move kilograms into orbit, SpaceX would be sufficient. None of these companies would, realistically, be able to match their economics. But that is not the goal, as we’ve already established.

Sovereign launch buys Canada and Canadian companies availability, timing, responsiveness, redundancy, and control. Think of it as an insurance policy: you pay for the peace of mind right up to when you need it, and then it pays for itself. The cost of funding these companies is, therefore, our “sovereignty insurance premium”.

Let’s roleplay: Imagine a Canadian Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance satellite covering the Arctic goes dark during a crisis.

How quickly can we replace it?
From which pad?
On whose rocket?
Whose approval do we need?

Right now, our only option is to ask someone else nicely. National defence strategy cannot be based on “Sorry to bother you, but could we squeeze onto your manifest?”

To be honest, the US would have every reason to help in such a situation due to NORAD — but that’s missing the point!

Maybe I’m biased because of the Canadian military uniform I wear, but “the Americans will probably help us” is not my favourite backup plan, and having spoken to quite a few of them recently, the Americans are a bit sick of it as well.

Europe has the same problems

Europe, like Canada, has excellent engineers, serious industrial capability, and considerably less scale than the US. It also has the remarkable ability to respond to urgent matters with seventeen committees that take twelve weeks to make six recommendations. European bureaucracy is a thing to behold.

Still, closer collaboration with Europe, in alignment with Prime Minister Mark Carney’s “Coalition of Middle-Powers” concept, could help increase the potential market for Canadian launchers, and find more launchers for Canadian launch-pads.

In late August, the European Space Agency signed its first European Launcher Challenge contracts, committing just under EUR 200 million each to Isar Aerospace, Rocket Factory Augsburg, and Spain’s PLD Space, with the explicit goal of reducing Europe’s dependence on a small number of launch providers.

That same Isar has signed a USD 113 million agreement for dedicated facilities in Nova Scotia, despite the fact that its Spectrum rocket has flown roughly 30 seconds and has not reached orbit. Through this deal, Europe gets geographic redundancy, useful polar and high-inclination orbit access, and lower reliance on American infrastructure. Canada gets revenue, range activity, and a German launch provider we did not exactly go shopping for — we went to Germany for submarines and came home with a rocket company. Is Germany just Costco?

Canada is also a part of NATO STARLIFT, an allied effort to enable short-notice launches (aka tactical launch) across a network of NATO spaceports and launch providers.

All good ideas, but at this time, all in PDF. We need launches!

The Horner Test

Let’s evaluate current Canadian launch options based on two simple questions:

  1. Is it operational today?
  2. Does it pass BGen Horner’s sovereignty test (i.e., can we use it without permission?)

Bottom-Line: Canada does not need a SpaceX, it needs independent launch options

Canada will not match American scale, neither will Europe, and we don’t need to. We can continue to use SpaceX when it makes sense, but we must build sovereign, Canadian launch capability as well.

Fund demonstrations, reward success, and have payloads waiting to fly when a Canadian launcher is ready.

We can continue carpooling with our neighbour when it makes sense, but he shouldn’t be the only guy in the parking lot with a car.
