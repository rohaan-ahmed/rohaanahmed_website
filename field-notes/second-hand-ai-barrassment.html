Second Hand AI-barrassment

The American midterm elections are turning AI into another team sport, complete with slogans, sponsors, and cheating. Canada and Europe should refuse to participate in this game, and instead take a more realistic approach to AI.

My current job title is Senior Manager of Artificial Intelligence, Cloud, and Data in a technology consulting firm. Prior to this, I was the Head of Advanced Technologies and Artificial Intelligence at a European space and defence company, with a team spread across UK, Spain, and Portugal. Prior to that, I was Product Manager for a space company in Canada building AI products.

This is not a LinkedIn post humblebrag. I just want you to understand that I know a little bit about what it’s like to work in, with, and for AI in both Canada and Europe, so you take me seriously when I say this: Regardless of where you stand on the “AI debate”,

Artificial Intelligence is too important of a technology to be decided by a bipolar American political system.

I don’t think there is much question anymore on the fact that AI is important. Whether it’s as important as the internet, the internal combustion engine, electricity, the wheel, language, or fire, remains to be seen. It also has very real and, perhaps, unprecedented costs. This issue must be debated logically, intelligently, and completely — not through political sound-bites, post-truths, and twitter posts.

The American midterms are coming for AI

Trump has recently come out gung-ho on Artificial Intelligence… or Super Intelligence (I can’t believe this is a real thing we have to deal with). He calls fears of AI destroying humanity a “hoax”, and claims those who speak out against data-centers and big-tech are simply falling for Chinese propaganda. Democrats, on the other hand, are using an overtly anti data-center stance to try and find votes in swing states. America is, therefore, turning a complex but important question into a test of which team you support.

I typically roll my eyes at most “both sides” arguments, but this time I’m going to make one. AI can do useful work, there is no doubt about it, but humanity also deserves protection. The build-out of AI, therefore, deserves nuance, not American jingoism.

Canada and Europe are uniquely vulnerable to American second-hand partisanship seeping into our political and cultural make-up — something Pierre Trudeau compared to “sleeping with an elephant”. The memories of the “COVID hoax” and “vaccine injuries” are only too fresh in my mind. There is a direct line, in my opinion, between Donald Trump’s nomination for the Republican party and Brexit — both campaigns were built on the same underlying principles, and both campaigns were immune to fact-checking.

Canada and Europe must not allow the AI debate in our countries to become a political tug-of-war. As big, powerful, and influential as it may be, let’s remember, America is a country that cannot reasonably regulate guns — a machine specifically designed to take lives — due to staunch partisanship. Can we really expect them to responsibly lead AI alignment regulation?

Use common sense

A data center is like a highway. It is neither inherently good nor bad. The questions around it should shape it: What will it provide? What will it consume? Who pays? Who benefits?

A few months ago, my cousin in England expressed that she is against data centers. When I asked her why, she listed the usual greatest-hits: water, electricity, pollution, and jobs. How much water does a data center use? No idea. How much CO2 is produced to power a server? Dunno. Eventually, she settled on “I just don’t want machines doing human things.” Valid point, I think, and an argument worth having, but significantly different from where the conversation started.

Europe had an EU AI Act in the works before it ever had a frontier model — a laughably European phenomenon. America, on the other extreme, has had AI models break out of isolation and hack other companies — something humans could go to jail for — without any real consequences for the labs. The best methodology is probably somewhere in between these extremes.

My ask

Both Canada and Europe must build within limits where the benefits justify the risk. Sovereign AI capabilities are going to be important — most of all for national security — therefore we must focus on local models running on local data centers that cannot be turned off whenever the Americans want. For every major data-centre proposal, demand a public account of what it does, who benefits, who bears the cost, what can go wrong, and what would cause us to say no. Then decide based on needs.

My cousin and I may still disagree, but we should not need an American election to dictate what we think.
