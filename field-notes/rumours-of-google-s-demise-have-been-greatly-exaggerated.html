Rumours of Google’s demise have been greatly exaggerated

Google’s biggest enemy in the the AI-race is Google itself, but even Google can’t stop Google from winning eventually.

Google’s biggest enemy in the the AI-race is Google itself, but even Google can’t stop Google from winning eventually.

Disclaimer: No AI was used in the writing, reviewing, or editing of this article. I did, however, ask Gemini which year it was first released, so there is a 10% chance it got it wrong.

In 2024 I lived in Silverstone, Northamptonshire, United Kingdom — a beautiful, sleepy, picturesque English village straight out of a children’s storybook that, for one week a year, turns into a bustling city when the Formula 1 British Grand Prix arrives. My typical running path, approximately 1.2 miles on Winterhills Road, was almost exactly half on a paved country road flanked by farmland, and half on a constantly damp dirt-track through an actual farm leading up to the race track.

Changing of the guard

It’s curious how sometimes you remember exactly what you were doing when you heard or saw something complete unremarkable. For example, I remember being on a run on this path on a beautiful summer’s afternoon when I heard about Google’s new AI model recommending putting glue on your pizza to keep the cheese from slipping off. The hosts of the podcast I was listening to — Hard Fork, a New York Time’s show about technology by two journalists who don’t really understand technology — were quite literally laughing at Google. Somehow, in what seemed the span of a few months, Google had gone from being the poster-child of technological innovation to a certain loser to OpenAI — and this was all before Anthropic had exploded onto the scene.

See, in 2024, the AI-race was already red-hot, and it was meant to have been fought between Google and OpenAI, with Meta being a distant third straggler. Anthropic was on nobody’s radar — it was seen as the AI company full of people who didn’t like AI — and was mentioned in the backpages alongside Mistral and Perplexity. No, everyone was just waiting for Google to absolutely annihilate the competition. It seemed inevitable.

Why wouldn’t they? Google had literally invented the transformer, the engine that powers LLMs. Google had all the data. Google had a massive, daily user-base. Google had the enterprise inroads. Google had their own AI-specific hardware (the TPU) and Cloud infrastructure. Google had the best programmers in the world. And, perhaps most importantly, Google had the money. There was no way Google could lose this, could they?

Except, the company known for user-friendliness and efficiency has never gotten out of its own way. The most stunning example of this has been the remarkably disjointed approach to branding and marketing. From Bard and Lamda(?) in its early days to Gemini, Gemma, Code Assist, Duet — there has always been a great amount of confusion about what each product actually does even amongst experienced practitioners (which I consider myself to be). It was also clear that, at least early on, Google saw Generative AI as a competitor to its existing cash cow, the 200 billion dollar search engine business — and so innovation was, perhaps, intentionally slowed down.

It is now the summer of 2026 — 2 years have passed since that summer run — which, in AI-years, is a century. Google is now competing with Meta to regain relevancy, while Anthropic and OpenAI lead the AI-race by miles. Whenever Google puts out a new model or a tool, it is met with, at best, lukewarm applause and, more often than not, a dismissive shrug. Absolutely nobody I know uses Gemini for anything, and I know a lot of AI users.

Beware of an old man in a profession where men die young

But here’s my hot take.

Google is down, but now out, because Google is extremely well diversified within AI.

Firstly, Google didn’t just enter the chip-making business as an afterthought, they have been doing this for a long time. Anthropic, the lab that replaced Google at the frontier, trains and serves its Claude models on Google’s computers. Unlike Nvidia, which is content with making Tier 2 and Tier 3 open-weight models, Google plans to compete at every Tier, whilst also controlling their own compute. So while its competitors have to rely on hardware providers (like Nvidia and Google), or setup their own nascent manufacturing (something OpenAI is looking to do), Google has a mature, well-established line that can serve it for decades to come.

Secondly, Google is the only one of the three cloud hyperscalers that is in the frontier conversation. Amazon is content being the serving layer, and Microsoft is putting its money into being the diversified, fine-tuning enterprise-focused AI company with its MAI line. That, essentially, leaves Google without a significant competitor. In fact, the cloud-battle itself shows that there may be no one clear winner, and that Google will always just hang around.

Third — data-centers. The thing everyone is scrambling to create is something Google has been building for 20 years. When it comes to scaling, Google is seasoned veteran.

Fourth, the active and engaged user-base is still a huge advantage. Android, Chrome, Search, Gmail, YouTube — all have millions of daily, active users. These are users who can and will interact with Google’s models, even if they don’t physically type “Gemini” into a bar. Despite the fact that Google cannot train its models on your emails (nor should it), it still gets access to metadata from millions of users on a daily basis from non-AI related activities. That’s the key difference — whereas the vast majority of Anthropic and OpenAI’s users are using their tool for the AI itself, Google’s users are simply using the internet, and the latter is a much, much larger group. This is an absolute goldmine for targeted training and monetization.

Fifth, Google’s political acumen will, in my opinion, prove to be a long-term asset. While Anthropic, OpenAI, Palantir, SpaceXAI etc. are in the news weekly for political reasons — whether for boardroom drama or fights with the government or just having a white-supremacist CEOs — Google flies under the radar. In fact, the only time I hear about their leadership is when one of them leaves, which may point to internal turmoil but is hardly rare in the industry. Google is adminstration proof, just like Microsoft, and this will pay dividends in the long term.

Lastly, Google has done this before. Historically, Google has rarely been the first to market — they have generally outcompeted incumbets. Chrome, Android, even Search (remember Yahoo?) — all are examples of Google coming from behind to eat the leader’s lunch. It is for the same reasons mentioned previously — they have the wealth to play the long-term attrition game, whilst having the talent to innovate late.

Ultimately, Google has the money, the chips, the infrastructure, the platform, the user-base, and the experience to win. In sports, there is a saying — don’t bet against greatness. If the definition of “win” is who will still be around in 10 years, and if I were a gambling man, I would bet on Google.
