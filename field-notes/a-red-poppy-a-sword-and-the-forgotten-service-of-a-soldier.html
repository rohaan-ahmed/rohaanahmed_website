A Red Poppy, a Sword, and the Forgotten Service of a Soldier

I came to Canada believing the poppy represented somebody else’s ancestors, somebody else’s sacrifices, and somebody else’s history. It took me years to discover that it represented mine, too.

Today, I serve as an officer in the Canadian Armed Forces, and every November, I willingly pip a poppy on my uniform’s lapel and parade on Remembrance Day. When I first arrived in Canada in 2003, however, I didn’t particularly care for the strange-looking red flower.

I was thirteen, in Grade 9, and wondering why my parents moved to a country where the air hurts my lungs. Every November, little red flowers would appear on people’s jackets, my school would organize a Remembrance Day assembly, and we would stand silently for two minutes while remembering soldiers who had died in wars I knew very little about.

I participated, of course. I stood when I was supposed to, remained silent when I was supposed to, and went back to class when I was supposed to — good practice for following orders later in my life.

I didn’t think remembrance was unimportant. I just didn’t think it had anything to do with me.

What I didn’t know then was that one of my own family members had served in the very war we were commemorating.

Learning about imperialism

As I grew older and learned more about colonialism, my indifference to remembrance gradually turned into resentment.

The poppy seemed to commemorate predominantly white British and Canadian soldiers who had fought in European imperial wars. My ancestors, meanwhile, had lived under the British Empire. I had difficulty reconciling those two things.

Why was I expected to commemorate the military history of an empire that had colonized India, much of the world, and profited from the transatlantic slave trade?

It didn’t help that some Canadians, as Don Cherry demonstrated years later, seemed determined to make wearing the poppy a litmus test of loyalty, suggesting immigrants who don’t wear one are somehow less Canadian. That sort of attitude was hardly going to convince me to wear one. If anything, it reinforced my resentment.

I suspect I wasn’t alone. Many Canadians whose families come from former colonies must have complicated feelings about Remembrance Day.

My own feelings had already begun softening as I learned more about Canadian military history, but toward the end of high school, something happened that gave me a much more personal reason to reconsider the poppy.

My grandmother’s visit

My grandmother visited Canada for the first time, which was also to be her last.

There is a joke amongst Gen Z that older generations sometimes drop life-altering lore casually on a random Tuesday. I lived through this exact situation.

While in the kitchen of our apartment, my grandmother told us about her father-in-law, my great-grandfather, a man I knew nothing about.

He had served in the British Indian Army during the First World War, and perhaps even in the Second.

He had deployed to Burma.

And his name was Captain Faqir Muhammad.

I was shocked.

At that age, I had no idea that soldiers from the Indian subcontinent had even participated in the Great War. It was not something that was taught in Canadian schools.

I was also confused: Why on earth would he fight for the British Empire?

Here was a man from a country colonized by the British, serving in the British Indian Army, fighting in a war whose causes and consequences were largely European.

Why?

Was it a career? Economic necessity? Family tradition? A sense of duty? Did he believe in the empire he served, or was the army simply a better alternative to toiling on a farm?

I still don’t know the answers.

British Indian soldiers in the Great War

The discovery led me to research the contributions of British Indian soldiers during the First World War, and what I learned fundamentally changed my relationship with remembrance.

Over 1.4 million soldiers and personnel from British India served during the Great War. They fought and served across Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. 74,000 died.

There are troops from my hometown in Punjab buried in North Africa whose graves may have never been visited by a descendant. Some of those descendants might now be living in Canada, attending the same Remembrance Day ceremonies that I parade in.

And these soldiers weren’t necessarily treated as equals.

The British Indian Army operated within a colonial racial hierarchy. Indian soldiers faced restrictions on advancement, unequal opportunities for commissions, and discriminatory treatment. Even their letters home were subjected to intensive censorship.

They served an empire that treated them as second-class soldiers. Whatever motivated them, their service and sacrifices were real. As real as their white counterparts from Britain and Canada.

Captain Faqir Muhammad

In Canada, it is common to hear stories about grandfathers and great-grandfathers who served in the World Wars. Their photographs hang in homes. Their medals are preserved. Their stories are passed down over family dinners.

In Pakistan, you rarely hear such stories about British Indian soldiers.

I suspect part of the reason is that remembering those soldiers means confronting the uncomfortable reality that they served a colonial power. Their service doesn’t fit particularly neatly into the patriotic stories we prefer to tell ourselves. Perhaps that’s why, in families like mine, those stories survived only in fragments, sometimes mentioned in passing in a kitchen.

“Part of the reason is that they were not fighting for their own country. None of the soldiers was a conscript. Soldiering was their profession. They served the very British Empire that was oppressing their own people back home.”
— Shashi Tharoor, “Why the Indian soldiers of WW1 were forgotten,” BBC Magazine, July 2015.

As a result, I know surprisingly little about my great-grandfather.

I know he served in the British Indian Army and was deployed to Burma at some point during the First World War, and perhaps even to Hong Kong. My grandmother recalled a story of him travelling through mountains with donkeys, perhaps transporting equipment or supplies.

There are also signs that he must have had a long and distinguished career — he rose to the rank of Havildar Major, roughly equivalent to a Sergeant Major, and received an honorary promotion to Captain prior to retirement.

We have an old photograph of him in his Captain’s uniform, his chest decorated with ribbons from a military career I have spent years trying to reconstruct.

And that’s about it.

We don’t know his regiment with certainty. We don’t know all the places he served. We don’t know the circumstances of his deployments, the men he served alongside, or the experiences he brought home.

What makes me particularly sad is how much he looks like my father in that photograph, and how little I know about him.

On the afternoon of 9 November 2026 I participated in a Remembrance Day parade in Milton, Ontario, Canada. The same evening, my father showed me a photograph of Captain Faqir Muhammad’s grave in Rawalpindi, Punjab Pakistan.

On the gravestone was his name, and two things that I had never known before: the date of his death, 28 October 1962, and his father’s name, Allah Ditta. After all these years I’m still discovering things about his life.

Someone had recently covered his grave in red rose petals, the same day I wore a red poppy on my uniform’s lapel.

This is what motivated me to write this article.

I wonder if he ever imagined that the hardships he endured, far from his family, might one day, in some small way, help make it possible for a country called Canada to welcome his great-grandson as one of its own.

Unlikely. But I like to think about it.

Because of him, somewhere along the years, the poppy stopped feeling like somebody else’s history. It had become mine, too.

Captain Rohaan Ahmed

In 2020, I joined the Canadian Armed Forces as a Reservist — first in the Army and then transferring to the Air Force.

By then, my relationship with the poppy had already changed. Joining the military didn’t convince me to embrace remembrance. My grandmother and my great-grandfather had accomplished that long before.

But wearing a uniform added another dimension.

Military service gives you a different appreciation for the people who came before you. To be honest, I can’t imagine the difficulties faced by troops travelling through Burma with pack animals a century ago. Rucking in the snow sucks even in 2026.

But I more clearly understand now that soldiers are ordinary people.

They have families, ambitions, fears, disagreements, and motivations that don’t always fit into the political narratives constructed around the wars they fight.

A soldier’s service doesn’t necessarily mean he supported every decision made by his government. It doesn’t mean he believed in every cause for which his army was deployed.

My great-grandfather’s service in the British Indian Army doesn’t require me to celebrate British colonialism.

And remembering Canadian soldiers doesn’t require me to agree with every war Canada has fought.

The Cavalry Sabre

For generations, a cavalry sabre that belonged to my great-grandfather has remained in the family. Whether it was his actual service sword or something he acquired later, I am not sure — what matters is that it was his.

It passed from him to his wife, and eventually to one of his daughters-in-law, my father’s aunt. In our culture, she is also my grandmother, with all the same love and respect that term deserves. And as is so often the case in South Asian culture, it was the women who made sure something precious survived.

She had looked after that sword for decades, and last year, she decided to gift it to me.

There are many grandchildren in our family, and I don’t take lightly the fact that she entrusted me with something so important to our entire family.

But she didn’t simply hand it over. She insisted that I put on my Canadian military uniform to receive it with the proper respect it deserves, in front of the entire family.

So I got my Commanding Officer’s permission and put on my uniform.

On the day, I marched up to her and saluted. She handed me the sword, with tears in her eyes.

“I am now giving you something that was a daughter to me, so take care of it…”

I don’t think I will ever fully understand the weight of those words.

She had protected this object for much of her life — it was her connection to a man who treated her like his daughter. And now she was giving it away.

I stood there in the uniform of the Royal Canadian Air Force, receiving the sword of a British Indian soldier who had served more than a century earlier.

I would never demand that another Canadian wear a poppy to prove they belong here. A Canadian who chooses not to wear one is no less Canadian than I am. I know, because I was once that Canadian.

But every November, I choose to wear mine. For the Canadians who served and sacrificed. For the British Indian soldiers whose stories have largely been lost to time. And for Captain Faqir Muhammad.

The empire he served is long gone. More than a century after his service, his sword now sits above a fireplace in Milton, Ontario, in the home of his great-grandson, a Canadian military officer.

Captain Faqir Muhammad has not been forgotten.
