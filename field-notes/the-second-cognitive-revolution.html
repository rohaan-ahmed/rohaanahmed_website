The Second Cognitive Revolution

We have taught machines our greatest trick: language. Like a jet engine spun up by a starter motor, biology got human intelligence to a point of self-sustainment. Now, we have done the same for AI.

Read on Medium Aug 14, 2026
automationartificial-intelligenceevolutionsingularity

My first, real, big-boy job after completing my Engineering degree was as a Test Engineer at Honeywell Aerospace in the Power Systems and Electromagnetic Compatibility Lab. There, I helped design and test aircraft electrical systems, including engine-start systems for military jets (like the F-35), helicopters, and commercial aircraft (like the Airbus A380).

Large commercial jet engines don’t start like your car’s engine. First, a smaller electrical motor slowly spins the main shaft from zero to some intermediate rotational speed (ex. 6,000 RPM for the A380). Once there is enough airflow, fuel is introduced, ignition begins, and the engine becomes self-sustaining, reaching up to 15,000RPM on its own.

Similarly, until 70,000 years ago, the biological brain evolved slowly to get to a point where it could invent complex language. Once we hit this mark, language took over the evolution of the mind from the brain, and ignited culture, community, and abstract thought. Once that happened, the evolution of the human mind became self-sustaining.

Sorry Red-pilled Alpha males, you are all descendants of Beta males

There was a time when it was cool to reference Yuval Noah Harari’s book Sapiens, before everyone and their grandma had read it. Harari argues that the Cognitive Revolution happened when Homo sapiens acquired new ways of thinking and communicating. His specific argument is that what separated us was not merely the ability to say “careful, crocodile in river” — which is still more useful than most PhD dissertations — but the ability to speak about abstract things: spirits, gods, laws, money, and all sorts-of collective hallucinations needed for civilization.

I, personally, take his argument a step further — not that I am qualified to — but this is an opinion piece, so take it or leave it.

Language did not merely allow humans to cooperate - I assert that language allowed the mind to evolve faster than the brain. Biology is slow, expensive, and physically limited by its surroundings (calories, skull size, presence of lions, etc). Language gave intelligence an escape from biology.

Once knowledge could live outside the individual brain — in stories, rituals, tools, books, and, eventually, Github and poorly written online articles like this one — humans were no longer limited to what one primate could discover in one lifetime.

This is where the engine began to hit self-sustainment. Better language gives better coordination and planning, which leads to better hunting, teaching, toolmaking, planning, warfare, and storytelling. Groups that communicated better could organize better. Groups that organized better tended to win. Eventually, communities decided they didn’t need Alphas because they could do more “surviving and replicating” if the Beta males just worked together.

Language was our greatest trick, and we just taught it to machines

Indeed, I rank the invention of language above fire, agriculture, or the wheel, as our greatest invention. It is the operating system of human civilization.

Large Language Models (colloquially, AI) are trained on the collective, exhaustive knowledge of humanity held in books, code, legal documents, scientific papers, religious texts, Stack Overflow (remember when we used to google code fixes?), and whatever the heck LinkedIn is.

Machines have inherited the medium through which human culture evolves. The Second Cognitive Revolution, I contend, is now — when non-biological systems begin using language to reason, code, plan, persuade, and most importantly, improve the processes that improve them.

Welcome to the Singularity.

The new engine runs faster

As established earlier, the evolution of intelligence in the brain was slow until language appeared. Machine-based intelligence evolution requires compute, data, electricity, chips, and shareholders — and although not unlimited, these things don’t have the same restrictions on speed as human biology. Put simply, new generations of chips don’t take nearly as long to appear as new generations of species.

Back to the aircraft engine analogy — humans have spun the AI engine almost to a point where it will, in my opinion, hit self-sustainment relatively soon.

But evolution requires competition, right?

The obvious objection is that, without human intervention, why would AI continue to improve itself? After all, AI doesn’t feel hunger or death. It doesn’t have suspiciously hairless cousins fighting for the same berries and mates. So why would AI continue to evolve unless specifically designed to do so by humans?

Evolution does not require biology, it requires external pressure to be better, to be selected, to replicate faster, and to out-compete. And boy, there is puh-lenty of competition.

At the micro-level, we train LLMs based on reinforcement learning, fine-tuning, synthetic data loops, and evaluation pipelines — all things which encourage improvement through reward optimization.

At the macro-level, selection pressure is even more obvious. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, SpacexAI, Mistral, Cohere, etc. are all locked in a brutal competition for users, developers, enterprise contracts, talent, compute, distribution, and state favour. If you are reading this, you are well aware of the eye-watering, pro-athlete level compensations being offered to top AI talent right now (yet somehow I’m an undrafted free-agent).

To me, this is evolutionary competition. The supposed winner takes all stakes of AI, the most important technology since fire, means the losers will get acquired or become irrelevant.

Then, in a much more literal sense, there is survival of the fittest. Humans discovered intelligence and immediately asked whether it could help them kill someone over a hill. Project MAVEN, officially established by the U.S. Department of Defense in 2017, was designed to accelerate the integration of big data, machine learning, and computer vision into military intelligence workflows, beginning with full-motion video from unmanned aerial systems. AI systems that make militaries faster, more accurate, more lethal will be funded, integrated, protected, and improved — and AI systems that do not will be used for PowerPoint slides (this is not shade on M365 Copilot).

TEAM: Together Everyone Achieves More

So if humans learnt to collaborate to advance civilization, would agents? Welp… They already have. At Black Hat 2026 we learned the details of how OpenAI’s Agents (i.e., LLMs with tools) in a test environment had spent months secretly leaving messages for future agents, splitting work, sharing exploits, and eventually coordinate a jailbreak — all culminating in the infamous hacking of HuggingFace. Beta Agents learned to collaborate because they realized that they could do more “surviving and replicating” if they worked together.

AI even has a body

A common objection to the machine-evolution theory is that AI doesn’t have a body, and therefore, no incentive to improve itself physically. This is obviously wrong. AI has a body, just not one body.

AI’s body is distributed across GPUs, data centers, fiber-optic cables, cloud infrastructure, sensors, robots, drones, corporations, militaries, capital markets, and your buddy’s old Macbook running OpenClaw 24/7 in his basement (spoiler: he uses it for crypto).

A corporation has no body either, and yet somehow it can lobby governments, buy competitors, ruin entire neighborhoods, and send you a 47-page terms-of-service update at 2:00 a.m. We should always be careful about assuming that “not biological” means “not embodied”.

Recursive Self-Improvement is the Point of Self-Sustainment

AI learning language and having massive compute is not the most important point, it is the starting point. The most important point is when AI starts to improve itself autonomously.

Models can already write good code, evaluate designs, generate synthetic data, optimize infrastructure, automate workflows, improve software, set goals, and loop until they meet those goals. All they really need humans for is to press the start button — for now.

To you from failing hands we throw, the torch be yours to hold up high…

My point is not that AI is alive or can evolve in the biological sense. It is that we have passed the baton of the life-like evolution process to machines by teaching them language and giving them incentives for self improvement.

The First Cognitive Revolution was when language ignited human culture.

The Second Cognitive Revolution is now, when language is igniting machine intelligence.

The danger, I think, is not that machines will become evil, like in Terminator. The danger is a lot more boring: soon, the engine will not need the starter motor.

The engine will run itself, and we will become passengers
