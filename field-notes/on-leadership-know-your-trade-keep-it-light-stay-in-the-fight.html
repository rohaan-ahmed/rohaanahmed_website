On Leadership: Know your trade. Keep it light. Stay in the fight.

My experience in civilian technical organizations and the military has convinced me that leadership is far less complicated than we make it: Understand the work well enough to build and lead a competent team. Keep that team motivated, engaged, and connected to the purpose of its work. And when things go wrong, keep finding practical ways forward.

My job as a leader in tech is to make myself largely redundant. If I get hit by a bus after a Monday meeting and my team doesn’t notice until Wednesday’s stand-up, I’ve done my job well. Unfortunate for me, personally, but good for my team.

This is only partially a joke. Under normal conditions, a good team shouldn’t require its leader’s constant presence — if every decision depends on my approval and every minor issue needs my intervention, then I’ve built the team wrong. When things go wrong, however, the value of good leadership should become unmistakable.

I’ve spent years leading technical teams of all sizes across engineering, software, artificial intelligence, defence, and space, as well as serving in the military. Some teams I’ve led have done really well, and others have taught me a little bit about what not to do.

Through these experiences, both as a leader and as someone who was led, I’ve built a leadership philosophy around three key principles:

  • Know your trade
  • Keep it light
  • Stay in the fight

1. Know your trade

I believe the commonly used phrase “management is a transferable skill” is only partly true. Yes, several leadership skills are transferable, but technical judgement and competence cannot be transferred via USB.

I have worked under non-technical project managers leading technical teams, and every technical conversation required translation, every decision needed an explanation, and the simplest problems could become an exhausting exercise in bureaucracy.

I’ve also worked under technically competent project managers, and the difference is remarkable.

Early in my career at MDA Space, I was working as a Systems Engineer on the European Space Agency’s (ESA) ExoMars rover. During qualification testing, I noticed that a wire leading to one of the wheel actuators would occasionally snag against the rover’s body. Nothing had broken yet, but repeated movement could eventually damage the wire. At that late stage of the design, changes generally required escalation, formal review, and formal approval from ESA. I brought the problem to our project manager, expecting a rather tedious process.

Instead, he immediately understood the engineering issue and, drawing on years of technical experience and project context, he suggested a minor upstream modification that solved the problem without triggering the formal escalation process. We reviewed it with the Systems Architect, who agreed with the approach, and implemented it within days.

In minutes, he had shown me that he understood both the engineering problem and the practical programmatic constraints, which earned my trust. I’m not alone in this — a study of British and American workers found that a boss’s technical competence is the single strongest predictor of job satisfaction (Artz, Goodall, Oswald, 2017).

Technical leadership is rather simple, but implementation is not always easy:

  1. Be technically proficient yourself,
  2. build a technically competent team,
  3. give them your intent and a clear end state,
  4. provide them the resources, and
  5. get out of their way, while remaining available for support.

(I just saved you a two-day leadership workshop)

The first point matters because it makes the others easier to do. This is the reason why “Achieve professional competence and pursue self-improvement” is the first point in the Canadian Armed Forces’ 12 Leadership Principles.

Don’t get me wrong, the leader need not be the best engineer, programmer, or scientist on the team. As responsibilities grow and your projects become more complex, you will inevitably lead people who know far more than you. You should understand enough to recognize expertise, ask intelligent questions, make informed decisions, and gain the team’s trust. Knowing nothing about the work is a bad place to start.

Personally, I have no desire to be the smartest person in every room. For one thing, it would severely limit the number of rooms I could enter.

2. Keep it light

The military understands something much of the corporate world doesn’t: Morale is a force multiplier.

Give me technically competent people who enjoy their work, trust each other and their leaders, and genuinely want to succeed, and a large percentage of management problems begin to take care of themselves.

I’ve experienced the opposite as well. I’ve worked under extraordinarily intelligent leaders who were extremely difficult to work with. Poor cooperation, unpleasant personalities, and constant friction can make a team of brilliant specialists ineffective — just ask Manchester United fans. I almost prefer the technically clueless manager I complained about earlier.

A collection of brilliant people does not automatically make a successful team. They must also feel empowered and be invested in each other’s success.

I was recently discussing Scott Snook’s book Friendly Fire with a group of MBA students. The book examines the tragic accidental shootdown of two American Black Hawk helicopters in 1994, and the organizational, procedural, and communication failures that contributed to the disaster.

The people involved were highly trained and individually competent, but, as Snook suggests, were a team in name only. Everyone assumed the overall situation was someone else’s problem, and decided not to intervene.

It’s an extreme example, and nothing like the problems of ordinary technical leadership, but it shows what happens when capable individuals don’t function as a team.

What most corporations get wrong is that they think morale means motivational speeches from the CEO, cheerful decorations, and the occasional free slice of pizza — although I am a huge fan of the last one. Fundamentally, it’s about people trusting one another, feeling comfortable challenging decisions, sharing a common goal, and knowing that hard work will be recognized.

Leaders also have a responsibility to make work interesting, not just track metrics. I know there’s no way to make data migration or ruck marches through four feet of snow “fun”, but you can explain the objective, not just the assignment.

Get to know your people as individuals. Understand what motivates them. Build genuine relationships without crossing professional boundaries. Keep a sense of humour. Don’t turn every mistake into a crisis. Share successes with your team, and own the failures yourself.

Keeping it light doesn’t mean lowering standards or avoiding difficult conversations.

When I left my last role at Indra Space in Oxford, UK, and moved back to Canada, I received the usual farewell messages from my team. What surprised me was that several colleagues continued reaching out for months afterwards, and some still keep in touch today. By then I was no longer their manager — I couldn’t influence their assignments, promotions, or careers. They had no professional reason to keep in touch.

I’d like to think it said something about the environment we had built together. We had our share of difficult technical problems, organizational bureaucracy, and daily frustrations, but perhaps we had also managed to make working together enjoyable.

Of course, it’s also possible they simply wanted to make sure I wasn’t coming back.

3. Stay in the fight

I realized fairly early that I wasn’t particularly blessed with what people call “natural talent”. Sports, technical studies, even writing (as you can probably tell) all seemed to require more effort from me than from other people.

That’s ok. Over time, persistence became a core part of my mindset.

Looking back, I attribute much of the success I’ve had to a stubborn refusal to stop trying. When something doesn’t work, I try something else. Often, I fail and learn. Occasionally, I succeed. The phrase “failing forwards” comes to mind.

The military has a useful expression for this: soldier on — accept the circumstances, work with what you’ve got, and keep moving. It is a core tenet of the Canadian Armed Forces Fighting Spirit.

I try to carry that attitude into technical leadership. Projects slip, requirements change, resources disappear, people disagree, and occasionally the brilliant solution from Monday creates three new problems by Friday.

What you must tell yourself and your team is that this is the point. There will always be more problems than time, money, or people to solve them. The leader’s responsibility is to prioritize what matters, commit resources accordingly, define clear goals, and keep the team moving. Sometimes that means fixing a problem, and sometimes it means deciding, deliberately, not to.

This is also why I favour evolution over revolution. Not every setback is a crisis, and not every process needs reinventing.

Sometimes large-scale or green-field change is necessary, of course, but there is a temptation in leadership to mistake dramatic action for effective action. Grand transformation initiatives attract attention, while quietly fixing small problems rarely receives the same recognition.

The size of the solution does not have to match the size of the problem.

All that to say…

When everything is running smoothly, a good leader should be almost invisible. When things go wrong, the value of good leadership should become unmistakable.

Know your trade. Keep it light. Stay in the fight.

And if I do get hit by that bus, I’d appreciate someone telling the team before Wednesday. Even if they don’t actually need me.
